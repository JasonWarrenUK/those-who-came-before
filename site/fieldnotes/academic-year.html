06 · Time and energy

Energy Runs Out Before Time Does

●●●●●stand

A career here runs four terms a year, twelve modelled weeks each: autumn, spring, a summer teaching term, and one summer research term. That structure was tested harder than almost anything else on this site, checked against a systematic sweep of alternatives and then stress-tested against seven separate scenarios of what an academic year can actually do to a person. It's held up. This page carries one thing that's deliberately still open, right at the end.

Two currencies, and energy is the one that actually binds

Time and energy are tracked separately, and it's energy, not time, that usually decides what you can actually get done. You can often fit five activities into a term on paper. Whether you can afford to do all five of them well is a different question entirely, and it's the one that actually matters.

Teaching never stops draining

Teaching costs energy every single week of a teaching term, regardless of what else you're doing that week. It's a background tax, not a scheduled cost. The one summer research term carries none of it at all, which makes it a genuinely different kind of season: fieldwork and sustained writing naturally cluster there, not because a rule forces them to, but because the economics of the rest of the year quietly push everything difficult into the one gap where it's actually affordable.

Promotion taxes the curiosity that earned it

Every step up costs you more background drain: a postdoc carries none at all, a junior lecturer a real but manageable amount, and it climbs steadily through senior lecturer, reader, up to a professor's much heavier load. Which means promotion is genuinely bittersweet: more prestige and institutional reach, and less free energy to actually spend on the research that reach was supposed to be for. Deliberately staying a postdoc longer than you need to, to keep that energy free, is a real strategic option, not a trap the game is hiding from you.

Push too hard and the work gets worse, quietly

Running at a deficit for several terms in a row accumulates energy debt, and work produced while in debt is measurably weaker: it argues less confidently, it's more error-prone, and it reads as a lesser form of document to whoever eventually judges it. Recovering means deliberately lighter terms, which means real opportunities passing by unused while you recover. There's no free way to have pushed hard and also not paid for it later.

Still open: does worry itself cost you anything?

This is the one piece we've deliberately left unwired. Should unresolved contradictions sitting in your queue, a pending crisis, general institutional anxiety, drain energy passively, on top of everything you're actively choosing to do? The places in the system where that could plug in already exist. Wiring it up either way is genuinely a small change to make. We just haven't decided yet whether a mind under strain should cost you energy simply for being under strain, or only for what you actively choose to do about it.
