02 · Being read

Read By Someone in Particular

●●●●○call

Peer review here isn't anonymous. That's a deliberate departure from how it mostly works today, and we've made the call, though the exact shape of the consequences is still being worked out in three directions at once: what it's like to be reviewed, what it's like to review, and what it's like to be questioned by someone who has no idea they're doing it.

From above: a reviewer with a memory

Whoever reviews your submission has a name, and their own published positions are things you can go and read. That changes the meaning of their verdict before you've even opened it. Acceptance from someone whose own work contradicts your claim carries real weight, because they had every reason to reject you and didn't. Acceptance from someone who already agrees with you was more or less expected, and counts for correspondingly less. Reviewers also remember: your last submission to them, whatever friction existed between your positions, whether you've publicly challenged their own work before. None of that resets between papers.

What we haven't settled: how far that memory should actually reach. A reviewer who remembers everything you've ever submitted starts to feel less like a colleague and more like an opponent with a filing cabinet. There's a version of this that's realistic and a version that's just punishing, and we don't yet know exactly where the line sits.

From across: the mirror

Sometimes you're the reviewer. You're handed someone else's paper about artefacts you've already examined yourself, and you get to watch their interpretive model diverge from what you believe the artefacts actually show, confidently and plausibly, the same way yours does when nobody's watching. This might be the moment a player first suspects the mechanic exists at all, not because anything explains it to them, but because they've just caught someone else doing the thing they've been doing the whole time.

From below: the question that lands by accident

A student asks you something about your own reasoning, and it happens to land squarely on the one step you've never actually defended, not because they were trying to catch you out, but because they were genuinely confused. That's a different kind of pressure to hostile review: nobody chose to attack you. Somebody was just honestly lost, and being honestly lost is sometimes the same thing as being right.
